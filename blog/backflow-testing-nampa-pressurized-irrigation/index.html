Backflow Testing in Nampa and Why Pressurized Irrigation Changes the Answer

Written by the Nampa Express Plumbing team · Published September 24, 2026

Backflow is the kind of plumbing topic most homeowners never think about until a notice arrives in the mail. In Nampa, the answer to whether you need backflow protection depends heavily on how your lawn is watered.

This guide explains what backflow is, why Nampa's separate irrigation system matters, when a device and annual testing are required, and how to protect an assembly through winter.

A quick way to know where you stand: walk to the spot where your sprinkler main leaves the house or valve box. A brass device with small screw-in test cocks is a testable backflow assembly. If there is none and your lawn runs only on canal water, you may not need one, but the sections below explain the exceptions.

The Basics

What Backflow Is and Why It Matters

Water normally flows one way, from the public supply into your home. If pressure drops in the supply, for example during a main break or heavy firefighting, water can be pulled backward. If a hose is sitting in a bucket of fertilizer or a sprinkler line connects to drinking water, that contaminated water can be drawn into the supply.

A backflow prevention assembly uses spring-loaded check valves to stop that reverse flow. Because the parts wear and foul quietly, the only way to know an assembly still works is to test it.

Nampa Specifics

How Pressurized Irrigation Changes the Picture

Most Nampa subdivisions water lawns from a canal-fed pressurized irrigation system that is entirely separate from drinking water. When a sprinkler system runs only on that irrigation water, it has no connection to the drinking supply, which removes the most common residential cross-connection.

The situation changes when a sprinkler system is fed from city drinking water, or when the two systems are connected anywhere, for example with a valve to switch a zone to city water when irrigation is off. Any connection like that needs backflow protection.

Kuna, for example, prohibits cross-connections between domestic and irrigation lines that do not meet its rules, and the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requires backflow protection for sprinkler systems connected to potable water.

Irrigation water only: usually no potable cross-connection.
Sprinklers on city water: protection required.
Any link between the two systems: protection required.

Winter

What to Do Each Fall to Protect a Backflow Assembly

  • 1Close the supply valve ahead of it. Use your own valve, never a city valve.
  • 2Open the test cocks. Turn the small test cock screws a quarter turn to let trapped water out.
  • 3Set the ball valves halfway. This lets water drain from inside the valve bodies.
  • 4Insulate or cover it. An insulated bag or cover helps on above-ground assemblies.
  • 5Test in spring if it froze. A cracked body or damaged check will fail its test. Repair, then retest.
Testing

How Annual Testing Works

Backflow assemblies in Idaho are commonly required to be tested once a year, as well as after installation or repair. A certified tester connects gauges to the test cocks and checks that each check valve holds and each relief valve opens at the right pressure. The results go to the water provider on the form they specify.

A failed test usually means replacing springs, checks or seals, followed by a retest. Our Nampa backflow page covers repairs and replacement, and our freeze damage repair page covers the damage that causes many spring failures.

Neighboring communities such as Star, where new irrigation connections are added constantly, follow the same annual routine.

Businesses

Backflow for Commercial Properties

Commercial buildings often have backflow assemblies on the main water service, fire sprinkler lines and equipment such as boilers, carbonated drink machines or chemical dispensers. These typically require higher-protection assemblies and annual testing.

Missing a test deadline can lead to notices from the water provider. Keeping test reports in one place and scheduling tests at the same time each year avoids that.

Businesses that run irrigation from city water also need protection on those connections, just like homes.

Spring Startup

What a Failed Spring Test Usually Means

Most failed tests trace back to a few causes. Knowing them helps you understand the repair quote.

Test resultLikely causeTypical repair
Check valve will not holdDebris on the seat, worn rubber, weak springClean or replace the check
Relief valve opens too early or not at allWorn diaphragm or seat on an RP assemblyRebuild the relief valve
Water leaking from the bodyFreeze crackReplace the assembly
Shutoff valve will not close fullyWorn ball valveReplace the shutoff, then retest

After any repair, the assembly is retested and the passing result filed with the water provider. Freeze cracks are the one failure that is entirely preventable with a proper fall drain-down.
