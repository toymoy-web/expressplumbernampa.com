Nampa Plumbing Emergency Checklist: Shutoffs, Water Heater and Irrigation Valve

Written by the Nampa Express Plumbing team · Published September 24, 2026

In a plumbing emergency, the first few minutes decide how much damage you end up with. The problem is that most people have never touched their main shutoff, and Nampa homes often have more than one system to think about.

This checklist is meant to be read before anything goes wrong. Walk through it once, find each valve, and the next emergency will be a lot less stressful.

It also helps to know what counts as an emergency. Water that is actively spreading, sewage coming into the house, no water at all in freezing weather, and any smell of gas all need action right away. A dripping faucet or a single slow drain can wait for a normal appointment, and treating them calmly saves money.

Do This Today

What to Do to Get Ready Before an Emergency

  • 1Find the main water shutoff. In many Nampa homes it is where the supply line enters the house, often in the crawlspace near the hatch, on a garage wall or in a utility room. Turn it once to be sure it moves.
  • 2Find the water heater's cold supply valve. It is on the pipe entering the top of the tank.
  • 3Learn the water heater's off setting. Gas units have a control knob with an off or pilot position. Electric units have a breaker.
  • 4Find your own irrigation shutoff. Know which valve is yours. The city's valve is for city use only.
  • 5Save the numbers. Your plumber, the gas utility and the city's water line should be in your phone before you need them.

Two Systems

Why Nampa Homes Need Two Shutoff Plans

Most Nampa homes have city drinking water and a separate pressurized irrigation system. Closing the main at the house stops the drinking water side but does nothing for an irrigation leak outside, and the reverse is also true.

Knowing which valve controls which system is the single most useful thing you can learn before an emergency. According to the City of Nampa, its 24-hour line at 208-468-5860 takes reports of irrigation leaks on the city's side.

If the leak is inside the house, it is the drinking-water side, and the house main is the valve you need.

Leak inside the houseClose the house main
Leak in the yard, meter movingClose the house main
Leak in the yard, meter stillClose your irrigation valve

After the Water Stops

What to Do Once It Is Under Control

Photograph the source and any damage before cleaning up, and keep every invoice. Most homeowner policies cover sudden water damage but not the failed part, and documentation helps either way.

Move valuables, rugs and electronics out of wet areas. If water soaked into walls, floors or the crawlspace, drying matters as much as the repair, and that is a separate restoration job.

For the repair itself, see burst pipe repair for split lines and fixing a failed water heater for tank problems. The same checklist applies across the area, including in Star and the other surrounding towns.

Renters and Landlords

If You Rent Your Home

Tenants should still learn where the main shutoff is and use it in an emergency. Stopping the water protects the property and your belongings, and landlords expect it.

After the water is off, notify the landlord or property manager right away, in writing if possible, with photos. Many leases spell out who calls the plumber. Keep a copy of any messages.

Landlords benefit from showing new tenants the shutoffs at move-in. A two-minute walkthrough can prevent thousands of dollars in water damage.

Make a Card

A One-Page Emergency Card for Your House

Write down, on one card or sheet, where the main water shutoff is, where the water heater's cold supply valve is, which breaker controls an electric water heater, where your own irrigation shutoff is, and where the gas meter is.

Add the numbers you would need: your plumber, Intermountain Gas, the City of Nampa's water line and your insurance company's claims line. Tape the card inside a kitchen cabinet or near the electrical panel.

Walk through it with everyone in the house, including teenagers and anyone who house-sits for you. In a real emergency, the person at home may not be the person who knows where the valves are.
