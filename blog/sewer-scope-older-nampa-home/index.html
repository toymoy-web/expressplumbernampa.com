Why You Should Get a Sewer Scope Before Buying an Older Nampa Home

Written by the Nampa Express Plumbing team · Published September 24, 2026

A home inspection checks the roof, the furnace, the outlets and the water heater. It does not look inside the pipe that carries everything from the house to the street. For an older home, that pipe is one of the most expensive things that can be wrong, and a buyer inherits it the day the sale closes.

A sewer scope, a camera run through the line, closes that gap. Home-buying advisories for Nampa commonly suggest one on houses more than about 20 years old. This guide explains what it finds and how to use it.

The Gap

What a Standard Inspection Misses

General home inspectors run water through fixtures and watch the drains. A partially blocked or damaged sewer line can still drain fine during a short test, especially in a vacant house that has not had a family using it for weeks.

The problems show up after move-in, when a household's daily volume hits a line with roots, a sag or a broken section. By then the repair is the new owner's bill.

Nampa's older neighborhoods near downtown were built across many decades, and some sewer laterals there are as old as the houses. Mature trees add root pressure on joints. Those are exactly the conditions a camera is built to check.

Findings

What a Sewer Scope Can Find and What It Means for a Sale

Here is how common findings typically translate into a buyer's decision.

The Process

How a Sewer Scope Works During a Purchase

The camera goes in through a cleanout, usually a capped pipe near the foundation outside, and travels to the connection with the public sewer. A transmitter in the camera head lets the operator mark the location and depth of any problem from the surface.

You get a recording and a written summary. That record is what lets you negotiate on evidence rather than suspicion, and it is useful to keep after closing as a baseline for future service.

Schedule it early in your inspection period so there is time to get a repair quote if something turns up. Our Nampa sewer camera service page covers the service in detail.

Buyer Checklist

What to Do Before and After the Scope

  • 1Ask the seller about the sewer. Past backups, root cutting or repairs are all worth knowing. Ask for invoices.
  • 2Confirm a cleanout exists. If there is none, the camera may have to go through a toilet, which the seller must allow.
  • 3Schedule inside your inspection window. Leave time for a follow-up quote.
  • 4Get a written quote for any problem found. A number on paper carries more weight in negotiation than a description.
  • 5Keep the recording. It documents the line's condition on the day you bought the house.

When the Scope Finds Trouble

A problem on the camera is not automatically a deal breaker. A single root-intruded joint is a modest spot repair. A long run of broken pipe is a full replacement. The recording and a quote let you and the seller agree on which one it is.

Our guide to fixing sewer lines explains spot repairs, lining and replacement. Buyers looking in older parts of Caldwell face the same question, since that city's core is similar in age.

Newer Homes

Is a Scope Worth It on a Newer House?

Less often, but sometimes. Construction debris, a joint that shifted as fill soil settled, or a sag from poor bedding can all show up in the first few years. If a newer home is still under a builder warranty, a scope before the warranty ends can document a defect while the builder is still responsible.

For homes over about 20 years old, it is one of the best-value checks a buyer can order.

Negotiation

Using Sewer Scope Results in a Purchase

When the scope finds a problem, buyers usually have three options: ask the seller to repair it before closing, ask for a price reduction or credit equal to a repair quote, or accept it and plan the repair themselves.

A seller-completed repair should come with an invoice and ideally a follow-up camera recording showing the fixed line. A credit gives you control over who does the work and how.

Either way, the recording and a written quote turn a vague concern into a specific number, which makes the conversation much easier for everyone.

Pipe Materials

Sewer Pipe Materials You May See on Camera

Older homes may have sewer lines made of materials that behave very differently as they age.

MaterialWhat to know
PVC or ABS plasticCommon in newer lines, smooth and resistant to roots at glued joints
Cast ironDurable but corrodes and scales inside over decades
Clay tileResists corrosion, but joints can let roots in
Mixed materialsCommon where part of a line was repaired, transitions can be weak points

The camera shows which material you have and where it changes. Knowing the material helps you understand whether a problem is likely to stay isolated or show up elsewhere along the line.
