Backflow Testing and Repair

Backflow Testing & Prevention in Nampa, ID

Backflow testing checks that the valve assembly protecting your drinking water still stops water from flowing the wrong way. In Nampa, Idaho, the most common reason is a sprinkler or irrigation connection, and the second is a commercial building's water service or fire line.

We test assemblies, repair or replace the ones that fail, and install new backflow prevention where a cross-connection needs it. You get the test results you need to file with your water provider.

Sound Familiar?

A Test Notice in the Mail, or a Valve Spraying in Spring

Backflow usually reaches people as paperwork or a leak. A letter says an assembly on your property is due for its annual test. Or the sprinkler system comes on in spring and water sprays from the brass valve beside the house. Or a new business owner learns the building needs a certified test before opening.

All three come back to the same device. A backflow assembly uses spring-loaded check valves to keep water moving in one direction only. When pressure drops on the supply side, for example during a water main break, those checks stop lawn water, fertilizer or building process water from being pulled back into the drinking supply.

Springs weaken, seals wear and debris lodges in the checks. Testing is the only way to know an assembly still works, because a failed one often looks perfectly normal.

Nampa Specifics

Why Pressurized Irrigation Changes the Picture

Most Nampa subdivisions water lawns from a separate pressurized irrigation system fed by canals, not from the drinking water line. On a home served that way, the sprinkler system usually has no physical connection to the potable supply, which removes the most common cross-connection.

Homes that irrigate from city water, or that have both systems tied together somewhere, are a different story. Any sprinkler system connected to drinking water needs backflow protection, a requirement under the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). It is worth checking which setup you have, because it decides whether a test notice applies to you.

The irrigation season normally ends in early October. Assemblies left full of water can crack when temperatures drop, and a cracked body fails its first test the following spring. Draining the assembly through its test cocks, or having it winterized, prevents that. The rest of the winter checklist is on our page about frozen lines.

Irrigation on canal water: often no potable cross-connection.
Sprinklers on city water: backflow protection required.
Every exposed assembly: drain before hard freezes.

Test Day

What to Do Before a Backflow Test

  • 1Find the assembly. Usually beside the house near the sprinkler main, or in a box or vault on commercial property.
  • 2Clear access. Trim plants and move stored items so the tester can reach both shutoff valves and all test cocks.
  • 3Have your notice handy. The water provider's notice lists the assembly, due date and where results go.
  • 4Expect a brief shutoff. Water through the assembly stops during the test, usually for a short time.
  • 5Plan for a retest. If it fails, repair and a second test are needed before results can be filed.
