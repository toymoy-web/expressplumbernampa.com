Who Owns the Pipe? Water and Irrigation Service Lines in Nampa

Written by the Nampa Express Plumbing team · Published September 24, 2026

A wet spot in the front yard raises an immediate question: whose problem is it? In Nampa, the answer depends on which pipe is leaking and where along it the leak sits, because most homes are fed by two separate systems with different owners.

This guide maps out who is generally responsible for each part, and how to figure out which pipe you are dealing with before you call anyone.

Getting this right matters for your wallet. Paying a plumber to fix a leak that sits on the city's side of the curb stop wastes money, and waiting for the city to fix a leak on your side just lets it run. A few minutes with the water meter usually settles which side you are on.

Two Systems

Drinking Water and Irrigation Are Separate

Nampa's drinking water comes from city wells through a metered service. Lawn water in most subdivisions comes from a separate pressurized irrigation system fed by canals, which is not metered and is billed by lot size.

Because the two systems are separate, they have separate lines into your property and separate points where responsibility changes hands. A leak on one has nothing to do with the other, and the first job is figuring out which is leaking.

For city-run pressurized irrigation, according to the City of Nampa, the city maintains pump stations, mains and curb stop valves in many subdivisions, and that the service line between the curb stop and the sprinkler head belongs to the property owner. The city will not repair a customer's service line. Where the Nampa & Meridian Irrigation District runs the system, the district maintains the main and the first valve off it, and the homeowner is responsible from there.

Which One Is Leaking

Using the Meter to Tell the Systems Apart

The drinking water meter is your best diagnostic tool. Close every fixture in the house and watch it. If it keeps moving, water is escaping from the drinking-water side somewhere past the meter.

If the meter sits still while a strip of lawn stays soggy, the leak is almost certainly on the irrigation side. In irrigation season that points to your service line or sprinkler system.

If you shut the main valve at the house and the meter still turns, the leak is on the buried line between the meter and your foundation.

Next Steps

What to Do When You Find a Leak

  • 1Do not operate city valves. Nampa asks residents not to touch its irrigation valves, which are for emergency use by the city.
  • 2Close your own valve. Use your house main or your personal irrigation shutoff if you have one.
  • 3Call the city if it may be theirs. Nampa's Water and Irrigation Division lists a 24-hour line at 208-468-5860 for irrigation leaks.
  • 4Call a plumber for private lines. Anything past the meter or curb stop on your side is a private repair.
  • 5Pinpoint before digging. Locating the leak first keeps the excavation small.

Getting the Repair Right

A leak on the drinking-water service line is handled on our service line repair page, and pinpointing a hidden leak first is covered on our page about finding hidden leaks.

Neighboring towns follow similar splits with their own providers. In Caldwell, the Caldwell Municipal Irrigation District runs pressurized irrigation for city subdivisions.

Before You Buy

Questions to Ask When Buying a Nampa Home

Ask whether the home has pressurized irrigation, who provides it and what the annual assessment is. Nampa bases its irrigation assessments on property square footage, and water is not metered.

Ask where the drinking water shutoff and the homeowner irrigation shutoff are, and whether a backflow device is on the property. Ask about any past service line leaks or repairs.

The Nampa & Meridian Irrigation District also advises buyers of new homes to ask about irrigation water availability. The answers tell you which lines you will own and maintain.

Winter Clue

Why Winter Makes Leaks Easier to Identify

Nampa's pressurized irrigation is off from roughly early October to mid-April. During those months, a wet spot or unusually green strip in the yard cannot be coming from an active irrigation line.

That makes winter a useful time to spot drinking-water service line leaks. If you see a patch of lawn that stays green, soft or icy while the rest of the yard is dormant, and the meter moves with all fixtures off, the buried line from the meter to the house is the likely source.

In summer, the two systems are harder to separate, which is when the meter test matters most.
